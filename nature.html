Our Nature

The story of Sri Lankan gems, from deep in the earth to the great collections of the world.

For more than two thousand years, this island has been known for one thing above almost anything else. Its old name, Ratna Dweepa, means the island of gems.

Made by the earth

Most of Sri Lanka's gems began in the Highland Complex, a belt of ancient metamorphic rock running through the middle of the island. Many of them formed roughly 540 to 610 million years ago, when great continental collisions heated and squeezed these rocks.

Over time, rain and rivers wore the rock away and carried the gems downhill. They settled in layers of gravel known as illam, and that gravel is where most Sri Lankan stones are found today.

An island in the old stories

Traders and travellers wrote about these gems long before modern gemmology began.

  1. The MahavamsaThe island's great chronicle tells of a Naga king with a throne set with gems.
  2. 1st centuryThe Roman writer Pliny the Elder records that envoys from the island, then called Taprobana, described its gemstones.
  3. 2nd centuryThe geographer Ptolemy mentions its beryl and sapphire.
  4. Around 1293Marco Polo writes of the island's sapphires, topazes, amethysts and other gems.
  5. 14th centuryThe Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta describes the variety of precious stones he saw.

Arab and Persian sailors knew the island as Serendib or Serendip, the name that later gave English the word "serendipity". The gem trade still uses another old name today: a Ceylon sapphire is a sapphire from Sri Lanka.

Gems of the island

Blue sapphire

The stone Sri Lanka is most famous for.

Padparadscha

A rare pink-orange sapphire. Its name comes from the Sinhala word for the lotus flower.

Ruby and spinel

Red and pink stones that were once easily confused with each other.

Cat's eye

A chrysoberyl with a bright line of light across its surface.

Moonstone

A soft, glowing stone long associated with the island.

Garnet, topaz and zircon

Part of a wide range of colours found in the same gravels.

How gems are mined

Much of Sri Lanka's gem mining is still done by hand. Miners dig a narrow pit, usually 5 to 25 metres deep, down to the gem gravel, then wash it to find the stones. In 2013, more than 6,000 of the country's mining licences were for this kind of traditional pit.

Ratnapura, whose name means city of gems, is the best known of the mining towns. Balangoda and Elahera are important too. The National Gem & Jewellery Authority issues the licences and holds deposits that help restore the land after mining.
